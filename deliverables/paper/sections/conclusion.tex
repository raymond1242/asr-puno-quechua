\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our submitted system fine-tunes the published XLS-R-300M checkpoint for 500
updates on the organisers' curated text, and improves the published baseline
from 13.15 to 9.14 mean WER under the established split. Most of this
improvement is orthographic rather than acoustic: the baseline's unchanged
hypotheses lose nearly eight points of scripted WER when scored against the
curated reference instead of the raw one. Systems on this benchmark should
therefore be compared only after training on the same reference text.

The established split shares every development sentence with training, because
the corpus records 2{,}067 sentences many times over, and selection on it can
mislead: a language-model weight chosen there recalls development sentences
from its training text and performs worse than greedy decoding on unseen ones.
We recommend reporting a text-disjoint figure alongside the conventional one,
together with per-speaker results, since with so few speakers a single voice
can dominate an evaluation partition.

We also report three approaches that did not help: logit averaging across
seeds, stronger masking, and language-model weights tuned on development data.
Weight averaging, external text for the language model, and propagating the
curated spellings to the spontaneous transcriptions remain untested.

\textbf{Future work for the community.} Building on the participatory work that
made this corpus possible, and to continue supporting the efforts on Puno
Quechua ASR, we suggest increasing the number of gold transcriptions from
elderly speakers. The spontaneous test consists entirely of speakers aged sixty
or over, while the corpus currently holds a single gold transcription from this
group, which makes systems for these speakers difficult to evaluate. Correcting
the existing silver labels for these speakers could be a practical first step,
and would help make this part of the benchmark measurable.
