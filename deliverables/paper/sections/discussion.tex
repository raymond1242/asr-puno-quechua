\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read-speech corpora for low-resource languages are built the way this one was:
a small pool of sentences recorded many times over by the few speakers who
volunteer. Any split that separates speakers while using all the recordings therefore
evaluates with complete text overlap, by construction rather than oversight:
a training portion that holds every clip also holds every sentence. We report
this as a property of the benchmark; a development set whose sentences the model has already heard from other speakers
can favour systems that remember text over systems that recognise speech.

Common Voice ships an official split for this corpus that separates speakers
and sentences at the same time, keeping one recording per sentence. We built
our own instead, so that every recording stayed available for training, but the
official one is the simpler route for anyone starting from scratch: it gives up
some training audio and returns a development set that memorised text cannot
flatter.

Decoding is the other place where the shape of the data decides the method.
Greedy CTC decoding commits to the most probable symbol at each 20\,ms frame
independently, with no notion of the word being formed, and the error profile
shows what that costs: on the baseline's out-of-domain set we measure 23.4\%
substitutions against almost no deletions or insertions -- the signature of
words that are nearly right. That invites a language model, and we chose one over
characters rather than words: Quechua builds words by chaining suffixes, and
77\% of the distinct word forms in the scripted sentence corpus occur exactly
once, so a word-level model would never have seen the correct form of most
words it would have to score.
